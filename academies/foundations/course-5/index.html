Foundations Academy · Lesson 5

Safety & Security

Safety at Hannah is not a binder on a shelf or a poster by the time clock. It is the sum of small decisions made by every Team Member on every shift — clearing a pathway, choosing the right glove, labelling a bottle, questioning a stranger in a restricted hallway, and speaking up the moment something looks wrong.

Estimated time 50–65 min Nine modules with hazard-spotting grids, PPE selection, chemical location checks, waste sorting, emergency practice cards, radiation hazard identification, a security-controls activity, and a safety walk reflection.

Lesson objectives

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Explain why safety is a shared responsibility held by every Team Member, in every role
  • Recognize the six common hazard categories found in a Hannah hospital
  • Select, don, and remove personal protective equipment for the exposure risk in front of you
  • Use labels, Safety Data Sheets, and approved dilutions before touching any chemical
  • Separate sharps, biohazard waste, contaminated laundry, and regular trash at the point of use
  • Apply the six universal emergency priorities to fire, facility, medical, and security events
  • Describe the time, distance, shielding, and monitoring principles that govern radiation safety
  • Protect restricted areas, credentials, records, and controlled substances without sacrificing hospitality
  • Report injuries, exposures, near misses, and unsafe conditions through the correct sequence
Prerequisites: Lessons 1–4. This lesson uses embedded knowledge checks within each module rather than a single final assessment, and closes with a safety walk reflection.

Module 1

Safety Starts With Me

Every safe hospital begins with one decision
I choose safety.

Safety is not limited to emergencies.

At Hannah, safety is reflected in everyday choices: keeping pathways clear, selecting the correct PPE, handling Pets within your training, protecting Member information, and speaking up when something does not look right. None of those decisions require a title or a certification — they require attention.

What Safety Protects

Pets

Prevent injury, escape, cross-contamination, and unnecessary stress.

Members and Guests

Maintain safe public areas, protect confidentiality, and communicate clearly during emergencies.

Team Members

Reduce exposure to sharps, chemicals, radiation, infectious material, bites, strains, and falls.

Hannah Facilities

Protect restricted areas, medication storage, equipment, keys, records, and emergency access routes.

Hannah Safety Principle — See something unsafe. Say something immediately. Do not assume someone else will report or correct the concern. Take accountability for making sure the right Team Member knows.

What You Will Practice

  • Recognizing common workplace hazards
  • Selecting and removing PPE safely
  • Using Safety Data Sheets and chemical precautions
  • Sorting sharps, biohazard waste, laundry, and regular trash
  • Responding to fire, facility, medical, and security emergencies
  • Understanding radiation restrictions
  • Reporting injuries, exposures, hazards, and near misses

Module 2

OSHA & Workplace Safety

What OSHA means in daily work
Recognize hazards before they cause harm, and follow established controls instead of improvising.

OSHA standards exist to reduce workplace injury and illness. At Hannah they are not paperwork — they are the reason a corridor stays clear, a container gets replaced before it overflows, and a Team Member stops rather than guesses.

Six Common Hazard Categories

Biological

Blood, urine, feces, saliva, contaminated bedding, infectious Pets, and laboratory materials.

Chemical

Cleaning products, disinfectants, medications, gases, and unidentified or improperly labeled containers.

Sharps

Needles, blades, broken glass, and any item capable of puncturing skin.

Physical

Wet floors, blocked exits, unsecured cords, defective equipment, noise, and escape risks.

Ergonomic

Unsafe lifting, awkward restraint, repetitive motion, and moving large Pets without enough help.

Behavioral

Ignoring warnings, rushing, bypassing a control, or performing a task outside your training.

OSHA non-negotiables. Use required PPE. Perform hand hygiene. Never place sharps in regular trash. Never push down an overfilled sharps container. Never mix chemicals unless the approved instructions specifically permit it. Report injuries and unsafe conditions immediately.

Module 3

Personal Protective Equipment

PPE is the last line of defense
PPE does not replace hand hygiene, safe handling, isolation practices, proper ventilation, or following the approved procedure.

Personal protective equipment reduces exposure when a hazard cannot be eliminated. It is the final barrier — not the first plan.

Common PPE and What It Protects

Gloves

Protect hands from bodily fluids, contaminated materials, certain chemicals, and medications. Change gloves between tasks and Pets.

Gown or protective clothing

Helps prevent contamination of scrubs and skin during isolation, heavy cleaning, or splash-risk tasks.

Mask or respirator

Used when the procedure or hazard requires respiratory protection. The correct type depends on the risk.

Eye or face protection

Protects against splashes, sprays, debris, and certain procedures.

Before putting PPE on. Know the task, identify the exposure risk, inspect the PPE, perform hand hygiene, and make sure the equipment fits correctly. Stop and ask for help when the required PPE is unavailable or you are unsure what applies.

Donning and Doffing

  • Example donning order: gown → mask → eye protection → gloves
  • Removal principle: remove the most contaminated items carefully
  • Avoid touching your face or clothing while removing PPE
  • Dispose of reusable and disposable items correctly
  • Perform hand hygiene immediately afterward

Module 4

Chemical Safety

Hazard Communication
Every Team Member who works around chemicals must know what the product is, what hazards it presents, how to use it safely, what PPE applies, and what to do after a spill or exposure.

Four Things to Check Before Using a Chemical

Label

The container must be clearly labeled. Never use an unknown or unlabeled product.

SDS

The Safety Data Sheet explains hazards, PPE, first aid, storage, spill response, and disposal.

Dilution and use

Follow the approved instructions. More concentrated does not mean safer or more effective.

Storage

Keep chemicals in approved areas and separate incompatible products.

Never mix cleaning chemicals. Mixing products can create toxic fumes, heat, splashing, or reduced effectiveness. Do not guess. Stop, move away from exposure, warn others, and follow the SDS and emergency procedure.

Spill or Exposure Response

  • Protect yourself and move away from the immediate hazard
  • Warn others and restrict the area when needed
  • Notify the appropriate leader immediately
  • Use the SDS for first aid, PPE, and spill instructions
  • Use the eyewash or other emergency equipment when indicated
  • Document the exposure or incident as required

Module 5

Waste, Sharps & Infection Prevention

Correct disposal prevents exposure
Waste must be separated at the point of use. Putting an item in the wrong container can expose another Team Member, contaminate clean materials, or violate safety requirements.

Waste Categories

Sharps

Needles, blades, and other puncture hazards go directly into the approved sharps container. Never recap unless a specific approved procedure requires it.

Biohazard

Items saturated with blood or other regulated biological material go into the designated biohazard container.

Contaminated laundry

Wear appropriate PPE, handle as little as possible, do not shake it, and keep dirty and clean workflows separate.

Regular trash

Only non-sharp, non-regulated, non-contaminated items belong in ordinary waste.

Sharps rules. Dispose of the sharp immediately after use. Keep the container upright and accessible. Do not reach into it, push contents down, or allow it to become overfilled. Report a puncture or exposure immediately.

Infection Prevention Basics

  • Use standard precautions with every Pet
  • Perform hand hygiene between tasks and Pets
  • Keep clean supplies away from dirty work areas
  • Use the correct cleaning product and contact time
  • Follow isolation signage and traffic controls
  • Ask for guidance before handling unfamiliar infectious risks

Module 6

Emergency Action Plan

Why the plan exists
An Emergency Action Plan gives Team Members a consistent way to recognize an emergency, protect people and Pets, communicate, evacuate or shelter, and account for everyone afterward.

Universal Emergency Priorities

1. Recognize

Identify the emergency and do not minimize warning signs.

2. Protect

Move yourself and others away from immediate danger when it is safe to do so.

3. Communicate

Notify the appropriate Team Member, activate alarms or emergency services when required, and use clear language.

4. Follow the plan

Evacuate, shelter, secure the area, or assist within your assigned role.

5. Account

Report to the designated rally point or status location so people and Pets can be accounted for.

6. Document

Complete required incident reporting and participate in follow-up.

Do not create a second emergency. Do not enter a dangerous area, attempt a task beyond your training, block an exit, or delay evacuation to collect personal belongings. Follow leadership and emergency responder instructions.

Common Hannah Emergency Types

  • Fire, smoke, or alarm activation
  • Power failure or critical equipment failure
  • Gas, chemical, or unknown fume concern
  • Earthquake or other natural disaster
  • Human medical emergency
  • Aggressive or escaped Pet
  • Security threat or suspicious person

Module 7

Radiation Safety

Why radiation safety matters
Radiation exposure is cumulative.

Safe radiography depends on trained personnel, restricted access, correct positioning, appropriate shielding, exposure monitoring, and avoiding unnecessary repeats.

Core Radiation Principles

Time

Reduce the amount of time near an active radiation source.

Distance

Increase distance whenever the procedure and assigned role allow.

Shielding

Use approved protective equipment and barriers correctly.

Monitoring

Wear the assigned dosimeter badge as directed and never share it.

Restricted access. Team Members who have not completed the required Radiation Safety certification may not enter the X-ray room during radiographic procedures. Observing from inside the room is still exposure.

Before an Exposure

  • Confirm that only authorized Team Members are present
  • Use the correct protective equipment
  • Position the Pet and equipment before activating the exposure
  • Keep doors and controlled areas secured
  • Avoid manual holding whenever approved alternatives are available
  • Report damaged PPE or missing monitoring equipment

Module 8

Security & Controlled Access

Security is part of safety
Friendly service does not mean unrestricted access.

Hospital security protects Pets, Members, Team Members, medications, equipment, records, and restricted workspaces.

Controlled Access Expectations

Visitors and Guests

Follow sign-in, escort, and restricted-area procedures. Do not allow someone to enter simply because they appear familiar.

Former Team Members

Past employment does not create current access privileges. They follow the same authorization requirements as other visitors.

Keys and badges

Keep them secure, report loss immediately, and never lend credentials to another person.

Passwords and records

Lock screens, protect login information, and discuss Member or Pet information only with authorized people.

Controlled substances

Maintain required storage, access, counting, and discrepancy-reporting controls.

Suspicious activity

Do not confront a threat beyond your training. Move to safety and notify leadership or emergency services as directed.

Challenge politely, escalate appropriately. A professional response can be simple: “This is a restricted area. Let me connect you with the appropriate Team Member.”

Module 9

Reporting & Safety Culture

Reporting prevents the next incident
Safety reporting is not about blame. It allows Hannah to provide care, preserve facts, correct hazards, identify patterns, and prevent recurrence.

What Must Be Reported

Injury or illness

Bites, scratches, strains, falls, needlesticks, chemical exposure, or any work-related symptom.

Exposure

Contact with bodily fluids, hazardous chemicals, medication, radiation concern, or contaminated sharps.

Near miss

An event that could have caused harm but did not. Near misses reveal risks before someone is injured.

Unsafe condition

Blocked exits, damaged equipment, missing PPE, overfilled containers, unlabeled products, or security concerns.

Reporting Sequence

  • Stop the task and obtain immediate care when needed
  • Make the area safe without putting yourself at additional risk
  • Notify the appropriate leader immediately
  • Preserve relevant information and do not alter the scene unnecessarily
  • Complete the required documentation promptly and accurately
  • Participate in follow-up and corrective action
Accountability & Follow-Through. Do not assume that mentioning a concern casually completes the process. Confirm that the right Team Member received the information and that immediate risk has been addressed.
