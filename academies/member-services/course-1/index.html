Member Services Academy · Course 1

Member Service Success

Connect, Listen, Respond

Every time a Member contacts Hannah, two things are happening at once. There is something to take care of — a refill, a question, an appointment, a result. And there is a person on the other side of it who loves their Pet and is deciding, in real time, whether Hannah is a place that listens.

This course is about doing both well: taking care of the problem and taking care of the person. You will work through six short modules, practice each skill as you go, and finish with a 10-question assessment and a teach-back with your leader.

Estimated time

50–60 minutes, including practice and the final assessment.

Who it is for

Service Coordinators first, and useful for every team member who talks with Members.

How you finish

Complete six modules, score 80% or higher on the final assessment, then complete the teach-back with your leader.

Where your work is saved. Your progress, reflections, assessment score, and teach-back notes are saved in this browser only. Reloading the page keeps them. Using a different browser, device, or private window starts over, and nothing is reported to a central training record. Ask your leader how your completion should be recorded.

Start with the objectives

Objectives

By the end of this course, you will be able to:

  • Explain why service quality decides whether a Member stays with Hannah, in your own words
  • Tell the difference between a Member's technical needs and personal needs, and respond to both
  • Name the five personal needs and recognize them in what a Member actually says
  • Open an interaction that is Clear, Caring, and Confident — the 3 Cs
  • Spot the behaviors that break listening, and repair them by paraphrasing and confirming
  • Choose between assuring, empathizing, and apologizing based on what the Member is feeling
  • Explain Pet health information in plain language, in small chunks, and check that it landed
  • Close a contact in a way that builds a lasting relationship — and know when to hand a situation to Hannah protocols or leadership

Scope of this course. This course teaches communication and service judgment. It does not set policy. It does not tell you what to say about pricing, billing adjustments, medical advice, diagnoses, treatment decisions, consent, documentation standards, or when to escalate a complaint. For any of those, follow the current Hannah protocol for your role and involve your leader.

Module 1

Why Member Service Matters

A satisfying Member experience is built from three habits: listening to what Members tell us, communicating clearly, and delivering quality service.

Listen to Member feedback

Ask for it, and take it seriously. Members tell us what they need and expect — usually before we ask.

Communicate clearly

A Member should understand what we offer, what happens next, and what they need to do.

Deliver quality service

Do the work well, and finish it. Follow-through is what turns a good interaction into trust.

Two sets of needs in every interaction

Member expectations rest on two sets of needs, and both are present in almost every contact.

Technical needs — taking care of the problem

The service, product, question, or problem in front of you: a refill to send, an appointment to book, information to give or gather, a result to explain. Hannah gives you tools, resources, and training to meet these.

Sounds like: “I need to reschedule Tuesday.” “What does the plan include?”

Personal needs — taking care of the person

How you relate to the Member as a person: making a connection, making them comfortable and confident, discovering all of their concerns, explaining clearly, and confirming they feel good about the experience.

Sounds like: “Nobody has explained this to me.” “I just want to know she's okay.”

The insight that holds the course together. Always solve the technical need. But how you treat the Member while you solve it is at least equally important. Long-term Member relationships are built on trust, care, and understanding — not on transaction speed alone.

Practice: technical, personal, or both?

Read what the Member says, then choose which need is in play. Some statements carry both. Choose for every statement, then check your answers.

1. “Bailey's medication runs out Saturday. Can I get a refill before the weekend?”
2. “I have explained this three times now, to three different people.”
3. “I just want to know that someone here actually cares about Luna.”
4. “Before I decide, what exactly does the plan include?”
5. “I am scared I am giving these at home wrong.”
6. “Nobody told me this visit would take two hours, and now I am late for work.”
7. “Please call me Mrs. Alvarez — not by my first name.”

Module 2

Meet the Person's Needs

Meeting and exceeding Member expectations is achievable, and it depends on how well you meet five personal needs. They are not personality traits — they are needs you can hear in the words a Member chooses.

The five personal needs

Open each one to see what it sounds like and what meets it.

To feel right

Members do not want to feel that a problem is their fault.

Sounds like: “I probably should have brought him in sooner.”
Meets it: “You brought him in — that is what matters. Let's look at where we are now.”

To express oneself freely

Members want to speak their mind and know they were heard.

Sounds like: “Can I finish?”
Meets it: Stop talking, let them finish, then paraphrase what you heard.

To be treated fairly

Members do not want to feel singled out, taken advantage of, or treated unjustly.

Sounds like: “Why did they get in today and I could not?”
Meets it: Explain honestly what you can, and never invent a reason.

To feel assured

Members want to know why you are asking a question or taking an action — and to believe you will take care of it.

Sounds like: “Why do you need all this?”
Meets it: Say why before you ask: “So the practitioner has the full picture, I am going to ask you three quick things.”

To be recognized as a unique individual

No Member wants to be treated like a number or a routine case.

Sounds like: “You called her ‘the schnauzer.’”
Meets it: Use the Member's name and the Pet's name, and refer to what you already know about them.

The four basic skills

Most successful Member interactions run through the same four skills. They are also the shape of the rest of this course.

  1. Greeting — set the tone in the first few seconds. Module 3
  2. Listening — understand what the Member wants and how they feel. Module 4
  3. Responding appropriately — answer the emotion and the problem. Module 5
  4. Ensuring lasting relationships — close in a way the Member remembers. Module 6

Practice: match the need

For each thing a Member says, choose the personal need behind it. Each need is used once.

1. “Why do you need to ask me all of these questions?”
2. “I was not finished — you cut me off.”
3. “My neighbor was seen the same day. I was told to wait a week.”
4. “I feel like I did something wrong by waiting to bring him in.”
5. “You keep calling her ‘the schnauzer in room 3.’ Her name is Luna.”

Module 3

Start Strong: Greeting

Members form an impression of you in the first few seconds — in person and on the phone. It comes from how you present yourself, your first few words, and the sound of your voice. That first impression is not permanent, but it does set the tone for everything after it.

A strong opening makes the rest of the interaction easier. A weak one means working harder for the rest of the contact to earn back ground you did not need to lose.

The 3 Cs: Clear, Caring, Confident

Clear

The Member immediately learns who you are and how you can help. Say the hospital, your name, and your role. If a Member asks you to repeat yourself, slow down.

Caring

Your tone and manner carry concern, so the Member feels they reached a real person. If your greeting sounds flat, smile — a smile is audible.

Confident

The Member believes you have the skill and knowledge to handle the situation. Say what you can do, not what you doubt.

Practice: build a greeting

Choose one option for each of the 3 Cs. Your greeting assembles as you choose. Then check it and see why each option works or falls short.

Clear — your opening words
Caring — how you invite them in
Confident — what you say next

Your greeting so far

Choose an option in each of the three sets above.

Reflection: your own greeting

Think about how you actually greet Members today. Consider your pace (not too fast, not too slow), your clarity (words pronounced fully, terms used correctly), and your tone (expressive, positive, matching your body language). Write one change you will make on your next shift.

Module 4

Listen for Meaning

Almost everyone believes they are an above-average listener, and Members still tell us that team members do not listen. For lasting Member relationships, no single skill matters more. Listening is how you learn what the Member wants and how the Member feels — both dimensions, every time.

Active listening has five moving parts: pay attention, show that you are listening, give feedback by paraphrasing and summarizing, hold your judgment until they finish, and respond in a way that shows you understood.

Seven behaviors that break listening

Open each one for what it looks like and how to repair it.

Interrupting the Member

Hard to recover from, and it reads as poor service. Repair: “I am sorry — I interrupted you. Please finish.”

Listening without full attention

Many roles at Hannah require multitasking; listening cannot be one of the tasks. The only thing to do at the same time is take notes on what the Member is saying.

Listening for facts without empathy

Facts matter, and so does how the Member feels. If you listen carefully you can hear both. Do not collect one and drop the other.

Thinking about what you are going to say

You think faster than people speak, so your mind races ahead to your reply. That is how you end up answering a question the Member did not ask.

Assuming you already know all the facts

You see similar situations constantly. Every situation is new to the Member. Confirm you have what you need before you act.

Jumping to conclusions

Listening does end in conclusions — but the goal is to take care of the problem and the person, which means bringing the Member with you to the same conclusion rather than announcing it.

Listening without participation

Silence does not feel like listening. Periodically show you are with them: paraphrase, confirm, or summarize what you heard.

Listening also tells you whether your explanation landed

Health literacy is a Member's ability to take in health information and make decisions with it. The practical instruction is to stop assuming understanding: gauge it by the language a Member uses and the questions they ask, and watch verbal and nonverbal cues for signs that someone is becoming confused or overwhelmed. Stop periodically and check (Shaw & Hunter, Clinician's Brief, May 2017).

The same article names quiet signals worth noticing: paperwork left blank or filled in incorrectly, handouts and discharge instructions that clearly were not read, and explanations like “I will read this later” or “I do not have my contacts in.” Treat those as an invitation to explain out loud, not as a Member being difficult.

Practice: find the listening breakdowns

Read the exchange, then select every behavior that is actually present. Leave the rest unselected.

Member: “Luna has been off her food since Thursday, and last night she—”

Team member: “Since Thursday, okay. So this is the same thing as last spring, that is usually just the food change. I will note it as a diet issue.” (typing, eyes on screen, does not look up)

Member: “I was going to say she also could not settle last night. She kept getting up.”

Team member: “Mm hm. So do you want the same appointment slot as last time?”

Which listening breakdowns appear in this exchange?

Practice: repair it with a paraphrase

Which paraphrase repairs the exchange best?

Stay in your lane on medical meaning. Repeating what a Member told you is good listening. Interpreting what a symptom means, or what should be done about it, belongs to the medical team under the current Hannah protocol for your role. When a Member asks you what something means, take the information accurately and route it.

Module 5

Respond and Recover

How you respond to the first thing a Member says shapes the whole interaction. Every response has to reach both sets of needs: the problem to solve, the question to answer, the service to arrange, the information to exchange — and the worry to settle, the frustration to ease, the fear to calm, the anger to cool.

Respond to the emotion first. A perfect solution delivered over the top of an upset Member rarely lands.

Three ways to respond

Assuring

Use it when no strong emotion is present. You tell the Member, simply and confidently, that you will take care of it.

“I can take care of this for you.” · “Let me get a little more information so I can help with this.”

Empathizing

Use it when the Member shows emotion — in either direction. You acknowledge and validate how they feel.

“I know how frustrating that is.” · “I do not blame you for feeling that way.” · And for good news: “That is wonderful — I am so glad to hear it.”

Apologizing

Use it when the Member has had an unpleasant experience. You acknowledge that it happened and express real concern. Apologizing puts you on the Member's side so you can work the problem together.

“I am sorry that happened.” · “I am sorry you have had this experience.”

One phrase to retire: “I'm sorry you feel that way.” It sounds like an apology and lands like a dismissal — it apologizes for the Member's reaction rather than the experience. Rewrite it as an apology for what happened plus empathy for the effect: “I am sorry this happened, and I understand why you are frustrated.”

What an apology is not. Saying you are sorry that something was hard is not an admission of fault, a promise of a specific remedy, or a decision about billing, credits, pricing, or care. Hannah has no complaint threshold, refund authority, or service-recovery script in this course. When a Member wants an outcome you cannot personally deliver, say what you can do next and bring in your leader or the current Hannah protocol for your role.

Practice: work a real situation

A three-step scenario. Choose a response at each step and read the feedback before you continue. You can restart at any time, and nothing is timed.

Step 1 of 3

Invite the Member's expertise

A Member knows their Pet better than anyone. Recognizing that expertise, and inviting their concerns, thoughts, opinions, and values, is part of responding well — and it produces information you would not otherwise get (Shaw & Hunter, Clinician's Brief, May 2017). Support their confidence with genuine reassurance rather than pressure.

“What are your greatest concerns?”

“You know Luna best — how do you think she will respond to this?”

“What matters most to you as we sort this out?”

“It will be hard the first couple of days, and I think you can do this.”

Module 6

Build Lifetime Relationships

In a service role it is easy to lose sight of what all the tasks add up to. Your task might be greeting Members, booking appointments, preparing a discharge, or sorting out a problem — but each one happens inside a larger goal: Members who stay with Hannah for the life of their Pet.

So at the end of every task, ask yourself one question: “What else could I do right now to make this relationship stronger?”

Closing a contact well

There are no strict rules for building relationships, but two guidelines hold: be positive, and be yourself. To the Member, you are Hannah. A close that reaffirms the connection puts your mark on the whole interaction.

  • Thank the Member for their Membership
  • Repeat your name, so they know who helped them
  • Ask whether anything else is unresolved — and mean it
  • Offer help in the future, and say how to reach you or the team
  • Be proactive: if there is something helpful and approved to offer — information, a class, a follow-up — offer it

Make sure it landed before they leave

Clear communication is not what you said; it is what the Member can now do. Four habits carry it, and they apply to a discharge conversation, a phone call, and a text message alike (Shaw & Hunter, Clinician's Brief, May 2017):

Plain language

Use everyday words instead of medical jargon. If a clinical term has to be used, say it and then say what it means.

Chunks of 1–3 sentences

Give a little, then stop. A paragraph delivered at once is a paragraph mostly lost.

Pause on purpose

Silence gives the Member room to process, form a question, or ask you to slow down.

Check comprehension

Ask often: “What questions do you have?” “How will you explain this to your family?” “How will you do this at home?”

Note the wording: “What questions do you have?” invites a real answer. “Any questions?” invites “no.”

Team handoffs are Member service too

A Member should never have to explain the same thing four times because a handoff was thin. A useful handoff names the Member and Pet, what was said, what was promised, who owns the next step, and by when. Team communication works on the same foundations as Member communication: know what is expected of each other, keep dialogue open, and respect different backgrounds and perspectives.

Practice: rewrite a Member message

A team member is about to send this to a Member after a visit, and to log it as a handoff note.

“Per the DVM, Luna's CBC/chem was largely unremarkable aside from mild elevations, so we'll plan to recheck in a bit and adjust the plan pending further diagnostics if clinically indicated. Someone will be in touch about next steps. Let us know if you have any questions.”

Select every problem with this message

Which rewrite is strongest?

Now write your own

Rewrite the message in your own words for a Member you actually talk to. Keep it plain, keep the chunks short, name who owns the next step and when, acknowledge how they may feel, and end with a real comprehension check. Do not add anything about what results mean.

Recap

What to carry onto the floor

Two needs, every time

Take care of the problem and the person. Emotion first, then the fix.

Five personal needs

To feel right · to express oneself freely · to be treated fairly · to feel assured · to be recognized as a unique individual.

Greet with the 3 Cs

Clear: hospital, name, role. Caring: warm, includes the Pet. Confident: what you can do.

Listen for both dimensions

What they want and how they feel. Full attention, no interrupting, no assuming, paraphrase before you act.

Choose your response

Assure when calm. Empathize when there is feeling. Apologize when the experience was poor. Never “I'm sorry you feel that way.”

Make it understandable

Plain language, 1–3 sentence chunks, pause, then “What questions do you have?” and “How will you do this at home?”

Close the loop

Thank them, repeat your name, ask what is unresolved, name the owner and the date of the next step.

Know the boundary

Pricing, billing, medical meaning, consent, documentation, and escalation follow Hannah protocol and your leader — not improvisation.

Final takeaway

Members do not remember the transaction. They remember whether they felt heard, respected, and confident about their Pet when they walked out. That is the part of the job only you can do.
